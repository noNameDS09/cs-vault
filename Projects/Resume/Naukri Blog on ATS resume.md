A good ATS score is generally 80 or above out of 100; this range typically indicates your resume is well-formatted for automated parsing, relevant to the role you’re applying for, and backed by specific, quantified content. A score below 60 usually signals real, fixable issues, most commonly around formatting or missing keywords, that are likely preventing your resume from even being read correctly by the system.

But a single number without context isn’t that useful; what actually counts as “good” depends on the range you’re in, what’s being measured, and where you are in your career. This guide breaks down the real benchmarks, what each range means in practice, and how to read your score instead of just staring at it.

## What Is an ATS Score?

An ATS score is a numeric rating, usually out of 100, generated by an [applicant tracking system](https://www.naukri.com/campus/career-guidance/applicant-tracking-systems-ats-software) or an [AI resume analyzer](https://www.naukri.com/campus/resume-analyser) to estimate how well your resume will perform when scanned by automated hiring software. It typically reflects a combination of:

- Parsing accuracy: whether the system can correctly read and extract your resume’s information
- Keyword and skill relevance: how closely your resume’s content matches a target role or job description
- Formatting compliance: whether your resume follows ATS-friendly structure and design conventions
- Content quality: how specific, quantified, and well-written your bullet points are

It’s important to understand that the real ATS used by an actual employer never shows you this score directly; you never see how a company’s specific ATS ranked you. What you’re seeing in an AI resume analyzer or resume score checker is a close simulation of that process, designed to flag the same kinds of issues before you apply.

## What Is a Good ATS Score? 

Here’s a general breakdown of what different score ranges typically mean, based on how most AI resume analyzers and resume score checkers structure their scoring:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Score Range**|**Label**|**What It Usually Means**|
|0–39|Needs Major Work|Likely significant ATS parsing or formatting issues — parts of your resume may not be read at all|
|40–59|Below Average|Readable, but missing key terms/skills, and content is likely generic|
|60–74|Average|Reasonable foundation, but specific, fixable gaps remain in keywords or content depth|
|75–89|Good|Solid, ATS-friendly resume that’s reasonably relevant and well-written|
|90–100|Excellent|Strong formatting, high relevance to target role, and consistently quantified content|

In short, anything below 60 is worth actively fixing before applying; 60–75 has clear room for improvement, and 75+ is generally considered a strong, application-ready resume.

## ATS Score Benchmarks by Experience Level

What counts as a realistic target score can also shift slightly depending on where you are in your career, since the content expectations differ.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Experience Level**|**Realistic Target Score**|**Common Score-Limiting Factor**|
|Fresher / Student|75–85+|Limited work experience to quantify; academic/project framing often too generic|
|1–3 years experience|80–90+|Job-hopping keyword mismatch; bullet points not yet outcome-focused|
|3–7 years experience|80–90+|Resume length creeping too long; older, less relevant roles diluting focus|
|7+ years/senior|80–90+|Overly broad content instead of role-specific, leadership-level keywords|

Freshers in particular shouldn’t be discouraged by a slightly lower starting score than an experienced candidate; the gap is usually closed through better framing of existing experience (internships, academic projects, extracurriculars), not by inventing experience you don’t have.

## What Actually Affects Your ATS Score?

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Factor**|**Typical Impact on Score**|**Example Issue**|
|Resume layout/format|High|Multi-column templates, text inside graphics|
|Section headings|Medium-High|Non-standard headings like “My Journey” instead of “Experience”|
|Keyword relevance to JD|High|Missing tools/skills explicitly listed in the job description|
|Bullet point specificity|High|Generic phrasing with no numbers or outcomes|
|File format|Medium|Heavily designed files, scanned images, unusual exports|
|Contact info placement|Medium|Details buried in headers/footers or inside graphics|
|Resume length|Low-Medium|Too short for experience level, or padded unnecessarily|

Formatting and keyword relevance tend to have the greatest impact because they determine whether your content is read and matched correctly in the first place; content quality matters just as much, but only once the first two are in reasonably good shape.

## ATS Score vs. Resume Score: Are They the Same Thing?

These terms are often used interchangeably, but there’s a subtle difference worth knowing:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Criteria**|**ATS Score**|**Resume Score**|
|What it measures|Specifically ATS parsing and keyword-matching compatibility|A broader evaluation, often including writing quality and structure|
|Who calculates it|Real ATS software during actual applications (not shown to you) or ATS-simulation tools|AI resume analyzers and resume-checking tools|
|Visibility|Never shown directly by real employer ATS platforms|Shown to you directly with a breakdown|
|Best used for|Estimating pass-through likelihood for automated screening|Overall resume quality check before applying|

In practice, most resume-checking tools use “ATS score” and “resume score” to describe the same general output: a number representing how ready your resume is for both automated screening and human review. What matters more than the exact label is understanding what’s being measured underneath it.

## Why Scoring Tools Can Show Slightly Different Numbers

If you’ve ever run the same resume through two different resume checkers and gotten two different scores, that’s normal, not a sign that one tool is “wrong.” Here’s why:

- Different tools weigh formatting, keywords, and content differently in their scoring models
- Some tools score against a specific job description, while others score generically
- Parsing engines vary slightly in how strictly they penalize certain formatting choices
- Some tools include grammar/readability in the score; others don’t

The practical takeaway: don’t fixate on hitting an identical number across every tool. Instead, look at the pattern of feedback; if multiple tools flag the same formatting issue or the same missing keyword, that’s the signal worth acting on.

## Do ATS Score Benchmarks Vary by Industry or Role Type?

The core benchmarks (75+ as good, 90+ as excellent) hold fairly consistently across industries, but what it takes to reach those numbers can differ slightly based on the type of role:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Role Type**|**What Tends to Raise the Score**|**Common Score-Limiting Factor**|
|Tech/IT roles|Specific tools, languages, and frameworks named exactly as in the JD|Listing skills too generically (e.g., “programming” instead of naming the language)|
|Sales/Marketing roles|Quantified outcomes — revenue, growth %, campaign metrics|Vague achievement language without measurable results|
|Design/Creative roles|Balance between a portfolio link and ATS-readable text content|Over-designed resume templates that hurt parsing|
|Operations/Finance roles|Process-specific terms and measurable efficiency gains|Long paragraphs instead of scannable bullet points|
|Campus/Fresher roles|Strong framing of internships, academic projects, and extracurriculars|Treating academic work as “just coursework” instead of quantified outcomes|

The scoring mechanics stay the same underneath: formatting, keyword relevance, and content quality, but which specific keywords or phrasing moves the needle will naturally differ by field.

## Real-World Example: Reading a Score Breakdown

Seeing a single number like “72” doesn’t tell you much on its own; the more useful part is the breakdown behind it. Here’s what a typical breakdown might look like for a resume scoring in the “Average” range:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Category**|**Score**|**What It Suggests**|
|ATS Formatting|85/100|Resume structure is largely fine; minor heading tweaks needed|
|Keyword Relevance|58/100|Several key skills from the target job description are missing|
|Content Quality|65/100|Bullet points are present but mostly generic, lacking numbers|
|Overall Score|72/100|Average — formatting is solid, but keywords and content need work|

In this example, the fastest way to move from 72 to 85+ isn’t touching formatting at all; it’s adding the missing keywords and rewriting a handful of generic bullet points, since those two categories are clearly dragging the average down. This is why looking at the category breakdown, not just the overall number, matters so much when deciding what to fix first.

## Does a High ATS Score Guarantee an Interview?

No — and this is worth being direct about. A high ATS score means your resume is significantly more likely to be correctly parsed, ranked favorably, and actually seen by a recruiter. It removes the risk of being filtered out for reasons unrelated to your qualifications.

What it does not do is evaluate whether you’re actually the right fit for the role, how you’ll perform in an interview, or whether your experience truly matches what the company needs beyond keyword overlap. Think of a good ATS score as clearing the first gate, not winning the race.

## Common Misconceptions About ATS Scores

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Misconception**|**Reality**|
|“The ATS score is the only thing that matters”|It’s a strong early filter, but interview performance and genuine fit still decide the outcome|
|“One low score means my resume is bad overall”|A low score is often driven by one or two fixable categories, not the entire resume|
|“A perfect score means a perfect resume”|A high score reflects strong ATS-readiness and relevance — it doesn’t evaluate tone, personality, or interview readiness|
|“The score is fixed once calculated”|Scores change immediately as you edit formatting, keywords, or content — it’s meant to be iterated on|
|“All resume checkers use the exact same scoring model”|Different tools weigh categories differently, which is why cross-checking the underlying feedback matters more than the number itself|

Understanding these distinctions helps you use your score as a diagnostic tool rather than either dismissing it entirely or treating it as the final word on your resume’s quality.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is considered a good ATS score? 

A score of 75 or above is generally considered good, with 90+ considered excellent. Scores below 60 usually indicate specific, fixable issues with formatting or keyword relevance.

### Is an ATS score the same as a resume score? 

They’re often used interchangeably. Technically, an ATS score refers more narrowly to parsing and keyword-matching compatibility, while a resume score can include broader factors like content quality, but most tools use the terms to describe the same overall output.

### Why do different resume checkers give me different scores? 

Different tools weigh formatting, keywords, and content differently, and some score against a specific job description while others use a generic scan, so scores can vary slightly across tools even for the same resume.

### Does a high ATS score guarantee I’ll get an interview? 

No. A high score means your resume is more likely to pass automated screening and reach a recruiter, but the interview decision still depends on whether your experience matches what the role requires.

### What’s a realistic ATS score target for a fresher? 

Freshers can realistically aim for 75–85+, even with limited work experience, by focusing on strong framing of academic projects, internships, and extracurricular achievements.

### Can a resume with great content still get a low ATS score? 

Yes. If the resume has formatting issues like multi-column layouts or text inside images, the ATS may fail to correctly parse the content in the first place, regardless of how strong that content actually is.

### How often should I check my resume’s ATS score? 

It’s a good idea to check before any major application push and again after tailoring your resume for a meaningfully different type of role or job description.

### Does resume length affect my ATS score?

Yes, though typically less than formatting or keywords. A resume that’s too short for your experience level may be missing relevant content, while one that’s too long can dilute your strongest points.

### Is it worth optimizing for a perfect 100 ATS score? 

Not necessarily. Scores of 90+ are generally considered excellent, and chasing an exact 100 across every tool can lead to over-optimization or keyword-stuffing that doesn’t reflect your genuine experience.

### What should I do first if my ATS score is below 60? 

Start with formatting; fix layout, section headings, and file format issues first, since these often prevent the rest of your content from being read correctly, before moving on to keywords and content quality.
